Mission Architect

========================================================================
SUMMARY   (197 words / 1180 chars - limits: 50-200 words, 1500 chars)
========================================================================
Mission Architect is a browser-based platform that puts you in the role of a NASA mission designer. Choose a science objective, select instruments, build a spacecraft, pick an orbit and a launch vehicle - then fly it. Every choice carries real engineering consequences: an array sized for Earth browns out at Mars, and an added instrument brings data you may not be able to downlink. Up to four players can hold four mission roles at once, in real time, each controlling only their own subsystem. The platform teaches one lesson: real missions are not designed to spend 100% of their budget. They hold 10 to 35 percent margin, because everything grows between design and launch. Designs are flown as a live simulation and validated against real coronal mass ejections recorded in NASA's DONKI database. All 10,368 spacecraft configurations are then enumerated to find the efficient frontier. The surface segment draws, to scale, the outpost your array, battery and link buy on the body you flew to, where landing twenty copies of your instruments still leaves one orbiter to carry the data home. Every number is derived from NASA documentation and linked to its source in the app.

PROJECT DETAILS   (8075 chars)
========================================================================
Mission Architect is an interactive mission design and validation platform. You choose a science objective, select instruments, build a spacecraft bus, pick an orbit and a launch vehicle, and then fly the result. One engine computes everything from your choices, and every failure reports the numbers and the way out rather than just saying no: "Array delivers 1620 W orbit-average at end of life; the spacecraft draws 1652 W (-2% margin)."

Three ideas carry the project.

1. MARGIN IS THE MECHANIC

Real missions are not designed to spend 100% of their budget. They hold 10 to 35 percent in reserve, because everything grows between design and launch: instruments get heavier, software gets hungrier, schedules slip and cost money. This platform makes that reserve the scoring mechanic rather than a hidden rule.

Each margin is graded. Below zero a mass, power or budget margin grounds the mission. Nought to 10 percent is RISKY and the first in-flight event will break you. Ten to 35 percent is NOMINAL and rewarded. Above 50 percent is OVERDESIGNED and penalised, because unused capability is not free: every spare watt is an array you paid to build, launch and carry for the life of the mission. The healthy band is drawn into every margin bar, so the thesis is visible before you read a word. We have not seen another entry model this.

2. FLOWN AGAINST REAL NASA DATA, NOT JUST CHECKED

A design is not a verdict, it is a mission. The flight console flies your spacecraft through its real orbit, dimming through eclipse, passing behind the planet, dropping a link to the ground station during a contact, while a strip chart plots array power, battery state of charge and recorder fill on one time axis. A scrubber runs from launch to end of life and re-flies the orbit at that array age.

Every series is a time-domain reconstruction of a number the engine already reported, and a test suite asserts exactly that, so the animation cannot drift away from the physics. It produces figures a static design tool cannot: a Mars design on an S-band link fills its recorder in 2.3 days and then loses 56.3 GB of science a day.

Designs are then validated against real coronal mass ejections from NASA's DONKI database, each with its measured speed and the spacecraft that observed it. A storm can end a mission three independent ways: insufficient power margin, insufficient data margin, or a single-event upset with no redundant attitude control. Orbit choice changes exposure by roughly five times, which is physically correct, because Earth's magnetosphere shields low orbits and nothing shields a lunar or Mars trajectory. DONKI sends CORS headers, so a static site queries it directly with no backend, and a bundled snapshot of the same real records takes over if the API is unreachable, so validation never breaks while the project is being judged.

3. NOT ONE DESIGN. EVERY DESIGN.

The engine costs 0.013 ms per run, which makes something unusual possible: instead of sampling the design space, we enumerate all of it. All 10,368 possible spacecraft buses are run in the browser in under 100 ms and plotted as science against cost, with the Pareto-efficient frontier drawn through them and your design marked on it. For the default objective, 76 of 10,368 fly: 0.7 percent. For a Mars design scoring 41, the platform reports how many configurations dominate it, shows a component-by-component diff, and adopts the better bus in one click.

WHAT YOUR DESIGN BUILDS ON THE GROUND

An orbiter is the first element of a programme, not the whole of it. The surface segment carries your array, battery, link, launch vehicle and instruments down to the body you flew to and draws, to scale against a 1.8 m crew figure, the installation they buy: solar farm, pressurised habitats, battery hall, ground terminal, radiators, volatiles plant, landing pads, radiation shelter. A day/night cycle runs with shadows that follow the Sun, and the orbiter you designed passes overhead and drops a relay link.

Nothing in that drawing is decoration. Every structure's dimensions come from a number the engine already reported, a table underneath prints the arithmetic behind every headline figure, and the eleven figures the mission physics cannot supply are published as declared assumptions rather than buried in an art asset. It produces the hardest lesson in the project: you can land twenty copies of your instrument suite and you still have one orbiter to carry the data home.

FOUR PEOPLE, FOUR ROLES, ONE SPACECRAFT

Mission design is an argument between specialists, so four people can have it. A session is created with a five-character code; each player takes one role and controls only that part of the spacecraft. The Mission Planner owns the objective, orbit and launch vehicle; Science owns the instruments; Engineering owns the bus; Operations owns lifetime and the decision to fly. You see the screens you control and the margins everyone shares, and a change touching a field you do not own is refused. Nobody can overrule anybody, which is exactly the constraint that forces a real trade discussion. A head-to-head mode gives every player the same objective and budget and ranks them at the end.

LEARNING BY FAILING, AND THEN KNOWING WHY

Three difficulty levels change how much the platform explains and how much reserve it demands, not the physics: Cadet explains every screen and softens events; Flight Director turns hints off and holds the design to a real programme's standard. After a run, a debrief shows where the score came from term by term, the margins thinnest first, what the trade study found that you left on the table, which real storm would have ended you, and ranked actions attributed to the role that answers for each. An in-app assistant, ArchitectX, answers questions about your own mission by calling the same engine the screens use, so it needs no API key, works offline and cannot invent a number. Designs save to a readable file and share as a link.

TOOLS AND ENGINEERING

React 19 and Vite 8, plain CSS, PeerJS for team sessions. A static site: no backend, no database, no accounts. Three self-hosted open-licence typefaces, so no third-party code loads at runtime. One engine owns every number and no screen calculates anything, which is why the simulation, the storm validation, the trade study and the surface segment cannot disagree with each other. Eight test suites gate every change, run by CI on every push, and the site is not allowed to publish unless they pass. The physics is standard and cited: inverse-square solar flux from 1361 W/m2, array degradation against a radiation index, the Tsiolkovsky rocket equation for propellant, a link budget scaled by transmit power and antenna aperture, and a cost model with a 1.35x integration wrap.

INTENDED IMPACT

Students learn orbital mechanics, spacecraft design and mission operations as three separate subjects and never see them interact. Change the orbit and the power budget moves. Add an instrument and the downlink fails. Hold too little margin and an ordinary solar storm ends the mission. No textbook shows that coupling. A simulator does, in seconds, as many times as you want to try, in any browser with no install, no account and no cost. It is built for a secondary school or first-year undergraduate classroom, where four students can take four roles and argue about one spacecraft.

AI ASSISTANCE

Two distinct things, kept distinct. Inside the product, ArchitectX is a keyword assistant, not a language model: no API key, no inference, no network, and it is labelled as such wherever a user meets it. During development, we used Claude (Anthropic) as a coding assistant: generating and reviewing module implementations, writing the test suites, and reviewing physics and copy. All engineering decisions, constants, NASA sources and calibration targets were chosen and verified by the team, and the engine is covered by eight test suites that must pass before anything publishes. AI use is permitted by the submission rules and we declare it rather than hide it.
