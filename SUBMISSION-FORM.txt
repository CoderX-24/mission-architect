PROJECT NAME

Mission Architect

========================================================================
SUMMARY   (197 words / 1180 chars - limits: 50-200 words, 1500 chars)
========================================================================
Mission Architect is a browser-based platform that puts you in the role of a NASA mission designer. Choose a science objective, select instruments, build a spacecraft, pick an orbit and a launch vehicle - then fly it. Every choice carries real engineering consequences: an array sized for Earth browns out at Mars, and an added instrument brings data you may not be able to downlink. Up to four players can hold four mission roles at once, in real time, each controlling only their own subsystem. The platform teaches one lesson: real missions are not designed to spend 100% of their budget. They hold 10 to 35 percent margin, because everything grows between design and launch. Designs are flown as a live simulation and validated against real coronal mass ejections recorded in NASA's DONKI database. All 10,368 spacecraft configurations are then enumerated to find the efficient frontier. The surface segment draws, to scale, the outpost your array, battery and link buy on the body you flew to, where landing twenty copies of your instruments still leaves one orbiter to carry the data home. Every number is derived from NASA documentation and linked to its source in the app.

PROJECT DETAILS   (15330 chars)

What it does

Mission Architect takes you through the phases of a real mission design: objective, payload, spacecraft, orbit, launch vehicle, simulation, trade study, space-weather validation and the surface segment the design makes possible. You pick from five science objectives, twelve instruments, eight subsystem groups, six orbits and four launch vehicles. At every step the platform recomputes the whole mission and tells you - in watts, kilograms, dollars, gigabytes and arcseconds - whether what you have built can actually fly.

How it works

One engine, `runMission`, owns every number. It takes a configuration object and returns the constraint checks, the margins, the in-flight events and the score. No screen in the interface calculates anything; the UI only renders what the engine returns. That single-source-of-truth design is why the simulation, the space-weather validation and the trade study cannot disagree with each other.

The physics is real and cited. Solar flux is 1361 W/m² scaled by the inverse square of solar distance, then reduced for packing and cosine loss, array degradation per year against a radiation index, and eclipse fraction. Propellant mass comes from the Tsiolkovsky rocket equation against a delta-v budget of insertion, station-keeping over the mission lifetime and end-of-life disposal. Downlink is a link budget scaled by transmit power, antenna aperture and an orbit-specific range term, integrated over ground-contact minutes per day and compared against what the instruments generate. Cost is instrument and bus cost with a 35% development wrap, plus the launch vehicle and annual operations.

It is a static site. No backend, no database, no accounts, no login - React and Vite built to GitHub Pages. Playing solo, the only call the page makes is to NASA's own API; a team session additionally connects to a public WebRTC broker to introduce the players to each other.

The margin mechanic

This is the heart of it, and we have not seen another entry model it. Real missions do not spend 100% of their mass, power, budget or data capacity. They hold a reserve, because everything grows between design and launch. So the platform grades the four margins it computes from your design \u2014 mass, power, budget and data: below 0% a mass, power or budget margin grounds the mission, 0 to 10% is RISKY and exposed to in-flight events, 10 to 35% is NOMINAL and rewarded, 35 to 50% is GENEROUS, and above 50% is OVERDESIGNED and penalised. You cannot win by hoarding and you cannot win by spending. The healthy 10-35% band is drawn directly into every margin bar in the interface, so the thesis is visible before you read a word.

Failure is never "mission failed". It is always the numbers and the way out: "Array delivers 1620 W orbit-average at end of life; the spacecraft draws 1652 W (-2% margin)."

Live mission simulation

A design is not a verdict, it is a mission. The flight console flies the spacecraft through its real orbit - one lap every twelve seconds, labelled with the true time compression - dimming through eclipse, passing behind the planet, dropping a link line to the ground station during a contact. Alongside it, a telemetry strip chart plots array power, battery state of charge and recorder fill on one shared time axis, with eclipse and contact periods shaded across all three lanes and the load line crossing the power trace.

A mission timeline then lets you scrub from launch to end of life and re-fly the orbit at that array age, watching degradation bite: the default design falls from 2940 W to 2492 W and its power margin from 65% to 58%.

Every series in the console is a time-domain reconstruction of a number the engine already reports - the mean of the array output equals the orbit-average power, the data rates integrate to the per-day figures, the time-domain battery verdict equals the analytic one. A dedicated test suite enforces exactly that, so the animation cannot drift away from the physics.

The reconstruction also produces a figure a static design tool cannot: days to fill the recorder. A Mars design on an S-band link fills its onboard recorder in 2.3 days and then loses 56.3 GB of science per day.

Space weather validation against real NASA DONKI records

Designs are flown against real coronal mass ejections from NASA's DONKI database. The bundled snapshot holds 433 actual CME records from 16 July to 13 November 2026, and a successful live call returns the most recent 60 days; each carries its measured speed and the spacecraft that observed it. DONKI serves CORS headers, so a static site queries the live API directly with no backend.

A storm's stress on a design scales with CME speed and the radiation environment of the chosen orbit, and it can break a mission through three independent paths: insufficient power margin, insufficient data margin, or a single-event upset with no redundant attitude control. Orbit choice changes exposure by roughly five times, which is physically correct - Earth's magnetosphere shields low orbits and nothing shields a lunar or Mars trajectory.

The live API is wrapped in a snapshot fallback of the same real records, so validation still runs if the API rate-limits or the venue network fails. We tested that path against a real HTTP 429.

Exhaustive trade study

`runMission` costs 0.013 milliseconds, which makes something unusual possible: instead of sampling the design space, we enumerate all of it. Every one of the 10,368 possible bus configurations is run in the browser in under 100 milliseconds, and the result is plotted as science score against cost with the Pareto-efficient frontier drawn through it and your design marked on it.

For the default objective, 76 of 10,368 designs fly - 0.7% - and two of those sit on the efficient frontier. For a Mars reconnaissance design scoring 41, the platform will tell you that 379 configurations dominate it and that 770 points of science are reachable for no more money, show you a component-by-component diff, and adopt the better bus in one click. Pareto-optimality is defined properly: a design is on the frontier when nothing dominates it, which a test asserts.

ArchitectX, and teaching without a language model

A question mark in the corner opens an intro for a first-time visitor, a filterable FAQ of twenty-five topics, and ArchitectX, an assistant that answers questions about the user's own mission. Asked why a mission does not fly, it lists the actual failing constraints in the engine's own words. Asked how to get more power, it runs the next array size up through the engine and reports what that upgrade does to power, mass and budget, and whether the result flies. Asked whether a design is any good, it reads the trade study and says how many of the 10,368 possible buses beat it for the same money.

ArchitectX is deliberately not a language model, and the interface says so in the box where you type rather than in small print. It matches a question against topics written by the team and then computes every figure by calling the same engine the screens use. That means it needs no API key, works with no network, costs nothing to run, and cannot hallucinate a number - a test asserts that the numbers in its answers match a freshly run mission, so the prose cannot drift away from the physics. For a teaching tool aimed at students, being unable to confidently state something false mattered more to us than sounding conversational.

Four people, four roles, one spacecraft

Mission design is an argument between specialists, so the platform lets four people have it. A session is created with a five-character code; each player takes one of four roles and controls only that part of the spacecraft. The Mission Planner owns the objective, orbit and launch vehicle; Science owns the instruments; Engineering owns the bus; Operations owns mission lifetime and the decision to fly. You are shown the screens you control and the margins everyone shares, and a patch touching a field you do not own is refused outright. Nobody can overrule anybody, which is exactly the constraint that forces a real trade discussion.

It runs with no backend and no accounts, like the rest of the platform. Two transports run at once: other tabs on the same machine sync through the browser's own BroadcastChannel, and other devices connect peer-to-peer over WebRTC through a free public broker. If the broker cannot be reached the session carries on locally and the interface says so rather than hiding it. A head-to-head mode gives every player their own copy of the same objective, same budget and same difficulty, and ranks them at the end.

Difficulty for different expertise

Three levels, because a student meeting orbital mechanics for the first time and an engineering undergraduate need different things from the same simulator. Cadet explains every screen, softens in-flight events and scores at 0.85x. Engineer is the calibrated default. Flight Director turns the hints off, draws five in-flight events instead of three, and holds the design to a real programme's standard: every margin above 10% and a 15% budget reserve, scoring at 1.25x. The physics is identical at every level; what changes is how much the platform explains and how much reserve it demands.

Post-mission debrief

A score is a number; a debrief is the reason for it. After a run the platform shows where the score came from term by term, the margins thinnest first, what the trade study found that you left on the table, which real DONKI coronal mass ejection would have ended the mission, what the design builds on the surface, and a ranked list of what to do next - each action attributed to the role that answers for it. In a team session players also rate and annotate the mission for each other inside the session, and a feedback form drafts a GitHub issue or an email for the user to send, since a site with no backend should never claim to have submitted anything.

Saving and loading a mission

Designs save to a readable JSON file on the user's own computer and load back with every identifier validated against the catalogs, so a file from an older build or an edited file is refused with a sentence explaining what is wrong rather than crashing the engine. The result snapshot inside the file is for human reading and is never trusted back in: the engine recomputes everything from the configuration.

The surface segment

An orbiter is the first element of a programme, not the whole of it, and this is the part of the platform that no other entry has. The surface segment takes the design off the drawing board and puts it on the ground: your array area and cell efficiency, your battery capacity and depth of discharge, your antenna, your instrument masses, your launch vehicle's capacity to that body and your mission lifetime are carried down to the surface of wherever you flew, and the installation they buy is drawn to scale - solar farm, pressurised habitats, battery hall, ground terminal, radiators, volatiles plant, landing pads, radiation shelter - against a 1.8 m crew figure for scale, under a day/night cycle with shadows that follow the Sun, while the orbiter you designed passes overhead and drops a relay link.

Nothing in that drawing is decoration. Every structure's dimensions come from a number the engine already reported, a table under the scene prints the arithmetic behind every headline figure, and the eleven figures the mission physics cannot supply - crew per module, kilowatts of life support, water extracted per kilowatt-hour - are published as declared surface assumptions rather than buried in an art asset. A test suite asserts that the farm area is units times array area, that available power recomputes from flux, efficiency and dust, that no two structures overlap, and that the same design always builds the same outpost.

Educational impact

Students learn orbital mechanics, spacecraft design and mission operations as three separate subjects and never see them interact. Change the orbit and the power budget moves. Add an instrument and the downlink fails. Hold too little margin and an ordinary solar storm ends the mission. No textbook shows you that coupling; a simulator does, in seconds, as many times as you want to try.

Because every entry in every catalog carries its NASA citation inside the app - visible in a sources panel, not buried in a README - a student can follow any number back to the document it came from. It runs in any browser with no install, no account and no cost, which matters for a classroom.

How we used NASA data

NASA's own guidance set the engineering frame: "Design and Develop Science Missions" and "CubeSat 101" for mission phases, margin practice and subsystem sizing. Real missions anchor the catalogs and give each design a flown analogue - Landsat 9, Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter (whose aerobraking approach to Mars orbit insertion the Mars case assumes), Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter, SPHEREx and DSCOVR. NASA DONKI, through NASA Open APIs, supplies the 433 real coronal mass ejections every design is validated against. NASA Mission Viz and NASA's own "Build It Yourself: Space Telescope" game informed how to present mission trade-offs to a learner.

Tools

React 19, Vite 8, plain CSS, gh-pages for deployment. Three self-hosted open-licence typefaces - Space Grotesk, Inter and JetBrains Mono - totalling 111 KB, so the site loads no third-party code at runtime, so nothing external can break how it looks or how it computes. Solo play calls one service, NASA's own API, and falls back to a bundled snapshot of the same real records if that call fails. A team session also uses a public WebRTC broker to introduce players to each other; if that broker is unreachable the session continues between tabs on one machine and the interface says so.

Creativity

Two choices we have not seen elsewhere. First, making engineering margin the scoring mechanic rather than a hidden constraint - rewarding the 10-35% reserve that real programmes hold, and penalising overdesign as well as recklessness. Second, exhaustive enumeration instead of advice: the platform does not tell you your design is suboptimal, it computes every alternative and shows you exactly which ones beat it and by how much.

AI assistance

Two distinct things, kept distinct. Inside the product, ArchitectX is a keyword assistant, not a language model: no API key, no inference, no network, and it is labelled as such wherever a user meets it. During development, we used Claude (Anthropic) as a coding assistant throughout: generating and reviewing module implementations, writing the test suites, and reviewing physics and copy. All engineering decisions, constants, NASA sources and calibration targets were chosen and verified by the team, and the engine is covered by eight test suites, which CI runs on every push and which must pass before the site is allowed to publish. AI use is permitted by the submission rules and we are declaring it rather than hiding it.
